Conjugate priors: the Gaussian cases, and where fat tails actually come from

The sequel to the Beta–Bernoulli note, from the same ML-foundations deck (FE5225, Bayesian Statistics → Gaussian Distribution, slides 27–42). Where that note had a single unknown bounded between 0 and 1, this one has two unknowns living on different domains, and the deck splits into three slide-groups that look like three unrelated recipes.

They are not three recipes. They are one likelihood, read through different variables. The shape you see — and therefore the conjugate prior you are allowed to pick — depends entirely on which parameter you hold still. That sentence is the whole chapter, and it is not written on any slide.

This note builds that claim from the bottom: first what the likelihood is as an object, then why it admits exactly three treatments, then each treatment, then what falls out at the end (a Student-\(t\), and a much smaller share of real-world fat tails than I first claimed).

Every number below was measured before it was written. The scripts are in figures/: verify_gaussian_conjugacy.py, verify_gaussian_predictive.py, session_surface.py, session_what_is_L.py, session_how_L_computed.py, session_slide38.py, session_two_sigmas.py, session_slide39.py, session_nonconjugacy_proof.py. Three claims I made and then measured turned out to be wrong; each correction sits with the claim it corrects rather than in a list at the end.

The symbols

  • \(\mu\) — the mean. \(\sigma^2\) — the variance.
  • \(\lambda = 1/\sigma^2\) — the precision. The deck switches to this early and never switches back; §"Why precision, not variance" says why.
  • \(\mathcal{D} = \{x_1,\dots,x_N\}\) — the data, \(N\) i.i.d. draws.
  • Conjugate prior — a prior whose family the posterior stays inside, so updating is arithmetic instead of an integral. Same meaning as in the Beta–Bernoulli note.

⚠️ The PDF's text layer mangles the Greek. Reading the slides directly, the substitutions are: ϑ² / ω² → \(\sigma^2\), ε → \(\lambda\), ϖ / ϱ → \(\alpha\) / \(\beta\) (the Gamma shape and rate), ↑ → minus, ↓ / ↔ → \(\propto\), !(·) → \(\Gamma(\cdot)\). Worth having beside you or the slides read as nonsense.

Three traps in this deck

Placed here, before the derivations, because you need them to read the slides at all. All three were verified by rendering the PDF pages as images — the text layer maps \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) to garbage, so "the slide says X" cannot be checked from extracted text. Two are outright errors; the third is not, which is what makes it dangerous.

Slide 35 drops a \(-1\) in the posterior exponent. It prints \(p(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D}) \propto \lambda^{N/2+\alpha_0}\); it must be \(\lambda^{N/2+\alpha_0-1}\), because the Gamma prior on the line directly above already carries \(\lambda^{\alpha_0-1}\). Checked against a brute-force posterior: the \(-1\) version matches Gamma\((\alpha_N,\beta_N)\) to 1.8e-10, the printed version is off by 5.3e-01. It also contradicts slide 36 outright — with \(\alpha_N=\alpha_0+N/2\) the exponent has to be \(\alpha_N-1\). With \(\alpha_0=2, N=3\) it inflates \(\mathbb{E}[\lambda\mid\mathcal{D}]\) by 29%. Caught by the reader, not by me.

Slide 40 writes \(\beta_N = \beta_0 + N/2\), dropping \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}\). Slide 36 has it right. This one also fails a units check — \(\beta_0\) carries squared data units and \(N/2\) is a pure number, so the two cannot legally be added. That is the fastest way to catch this class of error. Slide 40 is the starting point for the posterior predictive, so the mistake propagates into the Student-\(t\) scale.

\(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}\) silently changes meaning between sections. Not a typo — it is correct in both places, which is why nothing looks wrong. In the \(\mu\)-known section (slide 36) it is \(\frac{1}{N}\sum(x_n-\mu)^2\), against the known \(\mu\). In the general case (slide 38) it must be \(\frac{1}{N}\sum(x_n-\bar{x})^2\), against the sample mean, or the sum-of-squares identity fails. The reason is structural: an MLE is defined relative to what is unknown. With \(\mu\) known there is nothing to estimate for the mean; with \(\mu\) unknown you must estimate it first and everything reorganises around \(\bar{x}\). Reading slide 38, always take the \(\bar{x}\) version.

A general note on kernel-matching, since the first trap is an instance of it: \(\lambda^{a-1}\) is a notational convention, not content, which is exactly why the \(-1\) is easy to drop — it looks decorative but it is what makes reading \(a\) off a kernel work. Seeing \(\lambda^{k}e^{-c\lambda}\) means \(a = k+1\), \(b = c\).

Part 1 — The likelihood is one surface

Everything after this assumes you can picture a two-argument function as a surface and take slices of it. If that is already second nature, skim to Part 2.

Take three observations and nothing else: \(x = [1, 3, 5]\), so \(N = 3\). Small enough that every number below can be checked by hand.

The likelihood has two inputs, which in code is just:

def L(mu, lam):
    return lam**(N/2) * exp(-lam/2 * sum((x - mu)**2))

A one-argument function draws as a curve. A two-argument one cannot — it needs a table, and that table is the surface:

                mu=1        mu=2        mu=3        mu=4        mu=5
  lam=0.02    | 0.002316   0.002534  [0.002611]  0.002534   0.002316
  lam=0.05    | 0.006781   0.008492  [0.009154]  0.008492   0.006781
  lam=0.10    | 0.011633   0.018245  [0.021197]  0.018245   0.011633   <== SLICE 1
  lam=0.20    | 0.012105   0.029773  [0.040189]  0.029773   0.012105
  lam=0.40    | 0.004634   0.028031  [0.051076]  0.028031   0.004634
                                  ^^^^^^^^^^ SLICE 2

25 cells, one function, called 25 times. A "slice" is not an exotic operation — it is a for-loop with one argument frozen:

[L(m, 0.10) for m in mus]     # a ROW    -> "sigma^2 known"
[L(3.0, l)  for l in lams]    # a COLUMN -> "mu known"

The proof that these are one object: read the two slices out as lists —

SLICE 1 (row)    : [0.011633, 0.018245, 0.021197, 0.018245, 0.011633]
SLICE 2 (column) : [0.002611, 0.009154, 0.021197, 0.040189, 0.051076]

0.021197 appears in both. It is the cell where the row and the column cross. They are not two formulas; they are two readings of one table.

The analogy that makes it obvious: implied vol is a function of (strike, expiry). Freeze expiry, walk strikes → the smile. Freeze strike, walk expiries → the term structure. Two completely different-looking curves, and nobody says implied vol has two formulas.

What a cell contains

Each cell is a candidate Gaussian, scored by how well it explains the data you actually saw. Concretely, for the cell \(\mu=3, \lambda=0.4\):

ask the candidate how dense it is at each observed point, then multiply.

density at x=1  =  sqrt(0.4/2pi) * exp(-0.2*(1-3)^2)  =  0.113372
density at x=3  =  sqrt(0.4/2pi) * exp(-0.2*(3-3)^2)  =  0.252313
density at x=5  =  sqrt(0.4/2pi) * exp(-0.2*(5-3)^2)  =  0.113372
                                        product       =  0.00324302

You multiply because the slides assume the \(N\) observations are independent. Collecting the three terms gives the slide's form — three \(\exp\)s multiply into one, which is where \(S=\sum(x_n-\mu)^2\) comes from:

S = (1-3)^2 + (3-3)^2 + (5-3)^2 = 8
lam^(N/2) = 0.4^1.5 = 0.252982        exp(-lam*S/2) = exp(-1.6) = 0.201897
                       product = 0.051076   <- the table value

The table value and the full product differ by exactly \((2\pi)^{-N/2} = 0.063494\), a constant containing neither \(\mu\) nor \(\lambda\). It rescales every cell identically, so it cannot move the peak or change a slice's shape — verified: argmax and normalised shape are unchanged with or without it. Every \(\propto\) in the slides means "I dropped a constant that has no parameter in it."

The peak of the whole surface is the MLE: here \(\mu_{\text{MLE}}=3\), \(\lambda_{\text{MLE}}=0.375\), matching the largest cell.

Likelihood is not a probability

Same expression, two readings, and only one of them is a probability distribution:

fix sweep what it is
density the parameters the data \(x\) a real p.d.f. — integrates to 1
likelihood the data \(\mu, \lambda\) a score — integrates to 0.0904

Both measured on this dataset. So \(L\) is not "the probability of the parameters" — it is an unnormalised score over hypotheses. Turning scores into probabilities is what multiplying by a prior and normalising does, i.e. Bayes. This is what slide 34's footnote is protecting against, and it is the only property conjugacy ever uses.

The two slices, and why they differ

Row (\(\lambda\) frozen at 0.10): symmetric hill, peak at the sample mean, mirror image either side, defined for negative \(\mu\) too. A Gaussian shape.

Column (\(\mu\) frozen at 3.0): a wall on the left at \(\lambda=0\), long tail right, mean 0.625 vs mode 0.375. Not a Gaussian shape.

Walking down that column means walking from very wide candidates to very narrow ones, since \(\sigma = 1/\sqrt{\lambda}\) — precision up, width down, which is why it is called precision:

lam     0.02    0.10    0.40    1.00    4.00
sigma   7.07    3.16    1.58    1.00    0.50

Both ends of that walk fail, for different reasons: \(\lambda \to 0\) spreads the candidate so thin that producing exactly \([1,3,5]\) becomes vanishingly dense; \(\lambda \to \infty\) makes a spike at 3 that cannot account for \(x=1\) and \(x=5\). The optimum sits between, at 0.375.

Correction — why that slice is right-skewed. My first explanation blamed the decay rates and had them backwards. Measured, the right side decays exponentially (\(e^{-S\lambda/2}\)) and the left side only polynomially (\(\lambda^{N/2}\), hitting zero exactly at \(\lambda=0\)) — so the right decays faster in form and still owns the long tail. The real cause is available room: the mode sits at 0.375 with a hard wall 0.375 away on the left and nothing at all on the right. At 1% of peak height the left has 0.369 of room and the right has 1.813 — a 4.9× asymmetry. This generalises: any strictly-positive quantity whose mode is not near zero is right-skewed for boundary reasons, independent of its decay form.

The two shapes are genuinely different families, and that is not a curiosity — it is the constraint that drives everything in Part 2.

Part 2 — Why there are exactly three cases

There is only ever one likelihood:

$$L(\mu, \lambda) \;\propto\; \lambda^{N/2}\,\exp\!\Big\{-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\textstyle\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2\Big\}$$

Two unknowns in one expression. The conjugate prior you may choose is dictated by what shape this has as a function of the parameter you are inferring — and that shape is completely different depending on which one you fix:

Hold fixed What's left Family So the prior is
\(\lambda\) (σ² known) \(\exp\{\text{quadratic in }\mu\}\) Gaussian kernel Normal
\(\mu\) (mean known) \(\lambda^{N/2}e^{-\lambda\cdot\text{const}}\) Gamma kernel Gamma
neither the two are entangled Normal-Gamma Normal-Gamma

So the three slide-groups are three slices of one object. The first two pin a variable down so the expression degenerates into something recognisable; the third refuses to, and the coupling that appears is the actual content of the chapter.

Checked by scanning the same loglik(mu, lam) function along each axis and comparing to the claimed family:

as a function of mu   : max|L - N(xbar, 1/(N*lam))| = 7.088e-11
as a function of lam  : max|L - Gamma(N/2+1, S/2)|  = 4.554e-17

One function, two shapes.

The σ²-known case (slides 27–29) is derived in full in the sibling note, Gaussian conjugate priors: the posterior for an unknown mean — coefficient-matching through five steps, plus the "prior is \(\kappa\) pseudo-observations" reading that matches this note's \(N_0\). It is quoted here rather than repeated. The remaining two cases are derived below.

Part 3 — \(\mu\) known: the Gamma update

Step 1 — knowing \(\mu\) collapses the sum to a number.

$$L(\lambda) = \prod_n \sqrt{\tfrac{\lambda}{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_n-\mu)^2} \;\propto\; \lambda^{N/2}\,e^{-\lambda S/2},\qquad S = \sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2$$

This is the whole trick. With \(\mu\) known there is nothing unknown inside \(S\) — it is an ordinary number computed from the data, and the likelihood reduces to the single structure \(\lambda^{(\cdot)}e^{-(\cdot)\lambda}\).

Step 2 — recognise the Gamma kernel. A Gamma density is \(\mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda \mid a,b) \propto \lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda}\); matching gives \(a-1 = N/2\) and \(b = S/2\).

Step 3 — multiply by a Gamma prior and read off. With \(p(\lambda)=\mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda\mid a_0,b_0)\):

$$p(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D}) \;\propto\; \underbrace{\lambda^{N/2}e^{-\lambda S/2}}_{\text{likelihood}}\cdot\underbrace{\lambda^{a_0-1}e^{-b_0\lambda}}_{\text{prior}} = \lambda^{a_0+N/2-1}\exp\!\Big\{-\big(b_0+\tfrac{S}{2}\big)\lambda\Big\}$$

Exponents add, the structure is untouched, so it is Gamma again:

$$a_N = a_0 + \tfrac{N}{2}, \qquad b_N = b_0 + \tfrac{N}{2}\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}$$

Verified against a brute-force grid posterior (compute likelihood × prior pointwise, normalise): max|grid - Gamma(aN,bN)| = 5.898e-17.

📌 Easy to miss: \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum(x_n-\mu)^2\) divides by \(N\), not \(N-1\), and uses the known \(\mu\), not \(\bar{x}\). Bessel's \(N-1\) exists because estimating \(\mu\) by \(\bar{x}\) spends a degree of freedom. Here it isn't spent. (This is the third trap from the top of the note, in its first appearance.)

Why precision instead of variance

In \(\lambda\) the likelihood is Gamma-shaped — a clean exponential-family kernel \(\lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda}\). In \(\sigma^2\) the same object is inverse-Gamma: the same distribution, but the algebra is uglier, and the joint prior in Part 4 needs \(\lambda\) anyway to express the coupling. The substitution is bookkeeping, not insight.

What "\(a_0\) = effective prior observations" actually means

Slide 36 says observing \(N\) points raises \(a\) by \(N/2\), so \(a_0\) can be read as effective prior observations. I took that at face value and asserted it applies to \(\mathbb{E}[\sigma^2\mid\mathcal{D}]\). Measured, that is false:

n      E[var|D]      weighted avg   equal?
2      0.00022633    0.00018861     False
1000   0.00038796    0.00038720     False

The statement is exact for \(b_N/a_N\), not for the posterior mean of the variance. Setting \(N_0 = 2a_0\) and \(b_0 = \frac{N_0}{2}\sigma_0^2\):

$$\frac{b_N}{a_N} = \frac{N_0\sigma_0^2 + \sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2}{N_0 + N} \qquad\text{(exact to }10^{-15})$$

which is a textbook pooled variance: the prior contributes \(N_0\) pseudo-observations of variance \(\sigma_0^2\), weighted against the \(N\) real ones. The prior's pull is exactly \(N_0/(N_0+N)\) and vanishes on its own:

N        E[sigma|D]    sigma_MLE
0        0.01626       ---        <- pure prior
20       0.02018       0.02163
10000    0.02002       0.02002    <- data has taken over completely

Same shrinkage story as the Beta–Bernoulli note, and the same fix for the same failure: MLE on tiny \(N\) produces absurd variance estimates (at \(N=1\), exactly zero), and the prior holds it up until the data can stand on its own. Keep \(N_0\) in mind — it reappears in Part 5 as the Student-\(t\)'s degrees of freedom.

Part 4 — Both unknown: the coupling

The identity that makes it tractable

This is slide 38, and it rests on one algebraic identity worth deriving rather than quoting, because the move that makes it work is a term vanishing.

Step 1 — add and subtract the sample mean. Note \(\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \bar{x}\):

$$x_n - \mu = (x_n - \bar{x}) + (\bar{x} - \mu)$$

Nothing has changed, but the bracket is now data-only plus parameter-only.

Step 2 — square and sum. With \((a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\):

$$\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2 = \underbrace{\sum_n (x_n-\bar{x})^2}_{\text{(1)}} + \underbrace{2(\bar{x}-\mu)\sum_n (x_n-\bar{x})}_{\text{(2)}} + \underbrace{N(\bar{x}-\mu)^2}_{\text{(3)}}$$

Step 3 — term (2) is identically zero, because \(\sum_n (x_n-\bar{x}) = 0\) by the definition of the mean — it is the point where positive and negative deviations cancel. This holds for any \(\mu\) and any data; measured on three datasets the term came out 0, 0, and 5.2e-14 (float noise). That cancellation is the entire trick.

$$\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2 = \sum_n (x_n-\bar{x})^2 + N(\mu-\bar{x})^2$$

The "± a middle quantity so the cross term dies" manoeuvre is worth naming: it is the same one behind the bias–variance decomposition and the ANOVA sum-of-squares split. Recognising it once pays off repeatedly.

A corollary falls straight out: the second term is a square, so it is \(\ge 0\) and vanishes only at \(\mu=\bar{x}\). \(\bar{x}\) is the minimiser of the sum of squares — which is precisely why it is the MLE for \(\mu\): smallest sum of squares means least subtracted in the exponent, hence largest likelihood.

Step 4 — substitute back. Since \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n (x_n-\bar{x})^2\), the sum inside the exponential becomes two terms, so the exponential splits into a product:

$$L(\mu,\lambda) \propto \underbrace{\lambda^{1/2}e^{-\frac{N\lambda}{2}(\mu-\bar{x})^2}}_{\text{Gaussian in }\mu}\cdot\underbrace{\lambda^{(N-1)/2}e^{-\lambda N\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}/2}}_{\text{Gamma in }\lambda}$$

Look at the precision of that Gaussian: \(N\lambda\). It carries \(\lambda\) inside it. That single fact drives the rest of Part 4.

Seeing the coupling without algebra

You can see it on the same 25-cell table. Normalise each row to its own peak and the surface is a funnel:

  lam=0.02  |.................#######################################.................|  sd=4.082
  lam=0.05  |           .............#########################.............           |  sd=2.582
  lam=0.10  |                   .........#################.........                   |  sd=1.826
  lam=0.20  |                        ......#############......                        |  sd=1.291
  lam=0.40  |                            ....#########....                            |  sd=0.913

Every row is a Gaussian in \(\mu\) — but 4.47× wider at the top than the bottom. If \(\mu\) and \(\lambda\) were independent, every row would have the same shape and differ only by a scale factor, which is exactly what would let you write \(L = f(\mu)\,g(\lambda)\). They don't, so you can't.

Intuitively correct — noisier data means a worse fix on the mean. And note what it implies for Parts 1–3: freezing one variable is precisely the operation that hides the funnel. Locked to a single row you never see that the other rows have different widths. Each special case is correct and each one conceals the coupling, which is why they cannot be glued back together.

Constructing the Normal-Gamma prior

Slide 39 states the prior without motivating it. It is not derived — it is constructed, by a recipe worth stating explicitly:

Conjugacy requires prior × likelihood to keep the prior's form. So give the prior the same functional form as the likelihood, with the data-derived constants replaced by free parameters.

Reading them off the factored likelihood above:

in the likelihood → in the prior meaning
\(N\) (the multiplier on \(\lambda\) in \(\mu\)'s precision) \(b\) prior pseudo-observations
\(\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}\) (the centre) \(\mu_0\) where the prior puts the mean
\(N/2\) (the \(\lambda\) exponent) \(\alpha-1\) Gamma shape
\(N\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}/2\) (the \(\lambda\) coefficient) \(\beta\) Gamma rate

giving

$$p(\mu,\lambda) \propto e^{-\frac{b\lambda}{2}(\mu-\mu_0)^2}\lambda^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta\lambda} = \mathcal{N}\big(\mu\mid\mu_0,(b\lambda)^{-1}\big)\,\mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda\mid\alpha,\beta)$$

The \(\lambda\) inside \((b\lambda)^{-1}\) is not a trick — it is copied from the \(N\lambda\) position in the likelihood.

The posterior

Completing the square in \(\mu\) as before:

$$b_N = b + N,\qquad \mu_N = \frac{b\mu_0 + N\bar{x}}{b+N},\qquad \alpha_N = \alpha + \tfrac{N}{2},\qquad \beta_N = \beta + \tfrac{N\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}}{2} + \frac{bN(\bar{x}-\mu_0)^2}{2(b+N)}$$

Verified against a 2-D grid posterior: max|grid - closed form| = 2.220e-15.

\(\mu_N\) is the same weighted average as the σ²-known case, with \(b\) playing "how many observations the prior is worth". But the third term in \(\beta_N\) has no counterpart in either special case: it grows with \((\bar{x}-\mu_0)^2\), the disagreement between what the prior expected the mean to be and what the data says. Disagreement about the mean is itself evidence of larger variance. That term only exists because the two parameters are coupled, and it is invisible from inside either one-parameter case.

Proof: an independent prior is not conjugate

Slide 39 asserts this in one line. It is worth proving, because the proof shows exactly which feature breaks.

Proposition. Let the prior be \(p(\mu,\lambda)=\mathcal{N}(\mu\mid\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)\cdot\mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda\mid\alpha,\beta)\) with \(\sigma_0^2\) finite and free of \(\lambda\). Then the posterior is not in this family.

Step 1 — write the posterior. The prior's \(\mu\)-part carries no \(\lambda\):

$$p(\mu,\lambda\mid\mathcal{D}) \propto \lambda^{N/2+\alpha-1}\exp\Big\{Q(\mu,\lambda) - \lambda\big(\beta + \tfrac{N\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}}{2}\big)\Big\}$$

$$Q(\mu,\lambda) = \underbrace{-\tfrac{\lambda N}{2}(\mu-\bar{x})^2}_{\text{from the likelihood}} \underbrace{- \tfrac{1}{2\sigma_0^2}(\mu-\mu_0)^2}_{\text{from the prior}}$$

Step 2 — complete the square in \(\mu\). Collecting the \(\mu^2\) coefficients gives \(-\frac{1}{2}\big[N\lambda + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}\big]\), so the conditional is Gaussian with

$$\boxed{\;\tau(\lambda) = N\lambda + \tfrac{1}{\sigma_0^2}\;}$$

Step 3 — contradiction. The family's defining property is that \(\mu\) and \(\lambda\) are independent, so every member satisfies "\(p(\mu\mid\lambda)\) does not depend on \(\lambda\)". But \(\partial\tau/\partial\lambda = N > 0\), so \(\tau\) is strictly increasing and not constant. Hence \(p(\mu\mid\lambda,\mathcal{D})\) genuinely depends on \(\lambda\), so \(\mu\) and \(\lambda\) are dependent in the posterior, so no \((\mu_0',\sigma_0'^2,\alpha',\beta')\) reproduces it. ∎

The formula in Step 2 is the load-bearing claim, so it is measured directly — conditional variances read off a grid, no formula assumed:

     lam       measured   N*lam + 1/sig0^2      rel.err
    0.05       1.712500           1.712500     2.22e-16
    1.00       4.562500           4.562500     2.22e-16
   20.00      61.562500          61.562500     0.00e+00

Contrast the Normal-Gamma prior, where the same measurement gives \(\tau(\lambda)=(b+N)\lambda\):

     lam       measured      (b+N)*lam      rel.err
    0.05       0.350000       0.350000     3.33e-16
   20.00     140.000000     140.000000     0.00e+00

The entire distinction is whether \(\mu\)'s posterior precision has a constant term:

Normal-Gamma :  tau = (b+N)*lam        pure multiple of lam, factors out   OK
independent  :  tau = N*lam + 1/sig0^2 constant term, will not factor      breaks

And that constant term is the independence assumption: the prior claims \(\mu\)'s uncertainty is unrelated to \(\lambda\), so it contributes a fixed quantity to a precision that the likelihood contributes \(N\lambda\) to. The two are not the same kind of thing.

An equivalent test, useful because it needs no algebra: in any Normal-Gamma, \(\mathrm{sd}(\mu\mid\lambda)\cdot\sqrt{\lambda}\) must be constant. Measured, the Normal-Gamma posterior holds it flat at 0.37796 across \(\lambda\in[0.3,2.4]\) while the independent posterior drifts from 0.349 to 0.523.

The one exception: a flat prior

The proposition needs \(\sigma_0^2 < \infty\), and that condition is doing real work. As the prior flattens, the intercept vanishes and \(\tau(\lambda)\to N\lambda\) — which is a pure multiple of \(\lambda\):

    sig0^2    intercept    sd*sqrt(lam) at lam = 0.3 / 1.0 / 3.0      spread
         1    1.562500     0.34904  0.46816  0.53294                 1.84e-01
       100    0.010000     0.57417  0.57639  0.57703                 2.86e-03
     10000    0.000100     0.57732  0.57734  0.57735                 2.89e-05
 100000000    0.000000     0.57735  0.57735  0.57735                 2.89e-09

The invariant becomes constant in the limit, at \(1/\sqrt{N} = 1/\sqrt{3} = 0.57735\). So an improper flat prior on \(\mu\) is the one independent choice that does not break conjugacy — it is Normal-Gamma with \(b = N\). Non-conjugacy comes specifically from a finite, \(\lambda\)-free prior variance, not from independence as such. (Slide 43's improper priors are the natural next topic; see Status.)

Part 5 — The posterior predictive, and real fat tails

Every result so far is a distribution over a parameter. What you actually want is the next observation, so marginalise \(\lambda\) out — the same move as line 1 of the Beta–Bernoulli note:

$$p(x\mid\mathcal{D}) = \int \mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu,\lambda^{-1})\,\mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda\mid a_N,b_N)\,d\lambda$$

Collecting every \(\lambda\) term leaves another Gamma kernel, so the integral closes in one step via \(\int\lambda^{c-1}e^{-d\lambda}d\lambda = \Gamma(c)/d^{\,c}\):

$$p(x\mid\mathcal{D}) \;\propto\; \Big(b_N + \tfrac{(x-\mu)^2}{2}\Big)^{-(a_N+1/2)}$$

The shape changed. \(x\) was inside \(\exp\{-(\cdot)(x-\mu)^2\}\); now it sits in a negative power of a polynomial. Exponential decay became power-law decay — that single substitution is the entire fat-tail mechanism. Slide 42's reparameterisation names it:

$$x \mid \mathcal{D} \;\sim\; t_\nu\big(\mu,\; \text{scale}^2\big), \qquad \nu = 2a_N,\quad \text{scale}^2 = b_N/a_N$$

Verified against numerical integration: worst disagreement 4.594e-12.

Both parameters are quantities already derived in Part 3, which is the elegant part:

$$\nu = 2a_N = N_0 + N \qquad \text{scale}^2 = \frac{b_N}{a_N} = \text{the pooled variance}$$

Degrees of freedom = total effective sample size (prior pseudo-observations plus real ones). One number encodes how much you know, and it controls tail thickness directly: as \(N\to\infty\), \(\nu\to\infty\) and the Student-\(t\) becomes Gaussian — your ignorance about σ and the fat tail disappear together.

How much of the real fat tail this explains

Mid-session I said the fat tails in returns are "partly" caused by not knowing σ. The mechanism is real, but I stated the magnitude without measuring it. Measured:

window N   nu=N0+N   excess kurt   P(|x|>4sd)     [Gaussian = 6.3e-05]
3          13        0.6667        7.892e-04
10         20        0.3750        4.242e-04
60         70        0.0909        1.268e-04
250        260       0.0234        7.768e-05

Real S&P 500 daily returns run excess kurtosis ≈ 4–8. A 60-day vol window contributes 0.09. To reach 4 you would need \(6/(\nu-4)=4 \Rightarrow \nu\approx 5.5\) — a total effective sample of about five observations.

So parameter uncertainty explains on the order of 2% of the observed fat tail, not "part" of it. The other 98% is volatility clustering and jumps — real properties of the market, not estimation error.

One nuance worth not discarding with it: low kurtosis does not mean unchanged tail probability. At \(N=60\), \(P(|x|>4\text{sd})\) goes from \(6.3\times10^{-5}\) to \(1.27\times10^{-4}\) — it doubles. Kurtosis is a fourth-moment average and is insensitive to the far tail; for VaR-type work the doubling is the number that matters and the 0.09 would have talked you out of caring.

Why the mechanism is still worth having

Simulating it directly — draw \(\lambda\) from the posterior, then draw a return from that \(\lambda\):

N=3    simulated excess kurt =  0.639   theory 6/(nu-4) =  0.667
N=60   simulated excess kurt =  0.093   theory 6/(nu-4) =  0.091

Every single draw is Gaussian; the pile is not. This is a normal variance mixture, and it is the skeleton of stochastic-volatility models. The only difference is where the mixing comes from:

source of varying \(\lambda\) fat-tail magnitude
this chapter ignorance about one fixed σ dies off as \(N\) grows; ~0.09
SV / GARCH σ genuinely varies in time persists; reaches 4–8

So the derivation isn't wasted — swap "posterior over λ" for "λ that moves", and the same algebra produces the real thing.

Where this touches the trading work

The \(\mu\)-known case is not an academic simplification — it is what volatility estimation already does. Daily mean return is ~0.03% against 1–2% daily vol, so the mean is pure noise and essentially every vol estimator (EWMA/RiskMetrics included) sets \(\mu = 0\). That is slide 32's branch, not slide 27's.

What the Bayesian version adds over the MLE is a distribution for σ rather than a point, so "how wide is the 95% interval on my 60-day vol estimate" becomes answerable — a direct input to position sizing. Same theme as the volume-at-price work: stop shipping point estimates.

Appendix: a verification bug worth naming

The first run of the predictive script returned zeros from scipy.integrate.quad — the Gamma mass sits near \(\lambda\approx3000\) and the adaptive sampler over \((0,\infty)\) never found it. The script printed slide 42 confirmed anyway, because that print was unconditional and did not depend on the number above it.

This is the same defect as the launch-gate bug in the curtain-business repo, written by me this time, two weeks after documenting it. Fixed by making the claim contingent (assert worst < 1e-8) and by integrating over gamma.ppf(1e-13) to gamma.ppf(1-1e-13).

The rule generalises: any statement of success whose execution does not depend on the quantity being checked is not a check. Worth grepping for unconditional success-prints in anything that reports a verification.
