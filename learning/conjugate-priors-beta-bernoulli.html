Conjugate priors: the Beta–Bernoulli posterior predictive

A revision note built from working through one slide in the ML-foundations deck (Bayesian Statistics → Conjugate Priors). The slide derives, in four lines, the probability that the next coin flip is heads given the flips already seen, and ends "Exercise: compute the expectation in the last step." This note is me actually doing that exercise and pinning down why each line follows — not just nodding at it.

The one-line version: for a coin with unknown bias, the probability the next flip is heads equals the posterior mean of the bias — and with a Beta prior that mean is just \((m + \alpha) / (N + \alpha + \beta)\), a shrinkage estimate that blends the observed rate with a prior.

The symbols (the slide assumes them)

  • \(\mu\) — the coin's true, unknown probability of heads. This is the thing we are uncertain about. It lives on \([0, 1]\) because it is a probability.
  • \(\mathcal{D}\) — the data: \(N\) flips, of which \(m\) came up heads.
  • Prior — before seeing data we assume \(\mu \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\). The Beta is a distribution over \(\mu\) — a probability distribution over a probability. \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are its two shape knobs.
  • \(x\) — the outcome of the next flip. \(x = 1\) means heads.

The one fact that makes the whole slide collapse to arithmetic is conjugacy:

A Beta prior + Bernoulli/Binomial data → a Beta posterior. $$p(\mu \mid \mathcal{D}) = \mathrm{Beta}\big(\mu \mid m + \alpha,\; (N - m) + \beta\big)$$

You add your head-count onto \(\alpha\) and your tail-count onto \(\beta\). "Conjugate" literally means prior and posterior are the same family, so updating on data is addition instead of a hard integral. That is the entire reason the exercise is doable by hand.

The four lines

The target on the left, \(p(x = 1 \mid \mathcal{D})\), is not \(\mu\). We don't know \(\mu\). It's our honest prediction for the next flip that folds in all our leftover uncertainty about \(\mu\). It's called the posterior predictive.

Line 1 — marginalize \(\mu\) back out.

$$p(x=1 \mid \mathcal{D}) = \int_0^1 p(x=1 \mid \mu)\, p(\mu \mid \mathcal{D})\, d\mu$$

Two rules stacked. The product rule introduces \(\mu\) and integrates it away: \(\int p(x=1, \mu \mid \mathcal{D})\, d\mu = \int p(x=1 \mid \mu, \mathcal{D})\, p(\mu \mid \mathcal{D})\, d\mu\). Then conditional independence: once you know the true bias \(\mu\), the past flips tell you nothing extra about the next flip, so \(p(x=1 \mid \mu, \mathcal{D}) = p(x=1 \mid \mu)\). Read the line as: average the prediction over every possible \(\mu\), weighting each \(\mu\) by how plausible the data made it (that weight is the posterior). The \(\int_0^1\) runs \(0 \to 1\) because \(\mu\) is a probability and can't live anywhere else.

Line 2 — plug in the Bernoulli likelihood.

$$= \int_0^1 \mu\, p(\mu \mid \mathcal{D})\, d\mu$$

Because \(p(x=1 \mid \mu) = \mu\) by definition of a coin: the chance of heads given bias \(\mu\) is exactly \(\mu\). Nothing else moved.

Line 3 — recognize the integral as an expectation.

$$= \mathbb{E}_{p(\mu \mid \mathcal{D})}[\mu]$$

Pure definition: \(\int \mu\, p(\mu \mid \mathcal{D})\, d\mu\) is the mean of \(\mu\) under the posterior. The subscript just says "expectation under the posterior, not the prior." This is the slogan worth memorizing:

posterior predictive probability of heads = posterior mean of \(\mu\).

Line 4 — substitute the Beta mean.

$$= \frac{m + \alpha}{N + \alpha + \beta}$$

Mean of \(\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)\) is \(a / (a + b)\). The posterior is \(\mathrm{Beta}(m+\alpha,\, N-m+\beta)\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\mu] = \frac{m+\alpha}{(m+\alpha) + (N-m+\beta)} = \frac{m+\alpha}{N + \alpha + \beta}\). The \(-m\) and \(+m\) cancel in the denominator — that's why it lands so cleanly.

The exercise, done from the integral

The point of the exercise is to see why \(\text{mean of Beta} = a/(a+b)\) instead of taking it on faith. With \(a = m+\alpha\), \(b = N-m+\beta\):

$$\mathbb{E}[\mu] = \int_0^1 \mu \cdot \frac{\mu^{a-1}(1-\mu)^{b-1}}{B(a,b)}\, d\mu = \frac{1}{B(a,b)} \int_0^1 \mu^{a}(1-\mu)^{b-1}\, d\mu = \frac{B(a+1,\, b)}{B(a,\, b)}$$

The middle integral is, by definition, \(B(a+1, b)\). Expand with \(B(a,b) = \frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}\) and use the one identity \(\Gamma(z+1) = z\,\Gamma(z)\):

$$\frac{B(a+1, b)}{B(a, b)} = \frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\Gamma(a)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a+b+1)} = a \cdot \frac{1}{a+b} = \frac{a}{a+b} = \frac{m+\alpha}{N+\alpha+\beta} \qquad \blacksquare$$

So the two facts doing the real work are the Beta function \(B(a,b)\) (the normalizer that makes a Beta integrate to 1) and the Gamma recursion \(\Gamma(z+1) = z\,\Gamma(z)\) (the continuous version of a factorial). Everything else is bookkeeping.

Why \(\Gamma(z+1) = z\,\Gamma(z)\) — one integration by parts on the definition, so the note stands on its own.

Definition (for \(\mathrm{Re}(z) > 0\)): \(\Gamma(z) = \int_0^\infty t^{z-1} e^{-t}\, dt\), so \(\Gamma(z+1) = \int_0^\infty t^{z} e^{-t}\, dt\). Integrate by parts with \(u = t^{z}\) and \(dv = e^{-t}\, dt\) — hence \(du = z\,t^{z-1}\, dt\), \(v = -e^{-t}\):

$$\Gamma(z+1) = \Big[-t^{z} e^{-t}\Big]_0^\infty + z\int_0^\infty t^{z-1} e^{-t}\, dt$$

The boundary term is 0 at both ends — at \(\infty\), \(e^{-t}\) decays faster than any power \(t^z\) grows; at 0, \(t^z \to 0\) because \(\mathrm{Re}(z) > 0\) — and the leftover integral is exactly \(\Gamma(z)\), giving \(\Gamma(z+1) = z\,\Gamma(z)\). Differentiating the polynomial \(t^z\) is what manufactures the factor \(z\). Anchored at \(\Gamma(1) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t}\, dt = 1\), the recursion gives \(\Gamma(n+1) = n!\) — the continuous factorial. The two ratios above are just this identity read off: \(\Gamma(a+1)/\Gamma(a) = a\) and \(\Gamma(a+b)/\Gamma(a+b+1) = 1/(a+b)\).

Why the answer is worth memorizing: shrinkage

Rewrite the result as a weighted blend:

$$\frac{m+\alpha}{N+\alpha+\beta} = \underbrace{\frac{N}{N+\alpha+\beta}}_{\text{data weight}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{m}{N}}_{\text{MLE}} + \underbrace{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{N+\alpha+\beta}}_{\text{prior weight}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}_{\text{prior mean}}$$

It's a convex combination of

  • \(m/N\) — the raw observed frequency (the maximum-likelihood estimate, MLE), and
  • \(\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\) — the prior mean,

with weights set by \(N\) (how much data) against \(\alpha+\beta\) (how strong the prior). This is shrinkage: \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) act as pseudocounts — \(\alpha\) imaginary heads and \(\beta\) imaginary tails you pretend you saw before flipping. As \(N \to \infty\) the data drowns the prior and you converge to the MLE. As \(N \to 0\) you fall back to the prior mean.

Special case \(\alpha = \beta = 1\) (a uniform prior) gives \((m+1)/(N+2)\) — Laplace's rule of succession, the classic reason you never report a rate as a bare \(0/0\) or \(5/5\).

Trading hook

This is exactly how to estimate a win rate / hit rate from a small number of trades. Naive \(\text{wins}/\text{trades}\) on 12 fills is wildly noisy — \(8/12 = 67\%\) is not signal. A Beta prior centred on a skeptical base rate shrinks that toward something defensible, and \(\alpha+\beta\) is the dial for how much you trust the prior versus the fills. Same math underlies Beta-Bernoulli Thompson sampling if I ever bandit-allocate capital across strategies: keep a \(\mathrm{Beta}(\text{wins}+\alpha, \text{losses}+\beta)\) per strategy, sample from each, bet on the argmax.
