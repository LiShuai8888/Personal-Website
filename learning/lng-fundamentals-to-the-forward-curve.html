LNG fundamentals and the forward curve: a working framework

This is a working note, not a finished piece. It sets out how I am learning to turn LNG fundamentals, meaning which plants are producing, how much gas is in storage and where the cargoes go, into a view on the forward curve. The structure is here now; worked examples and charts will be added as I build each part.

The short version: every fundamental should end up as a fair value for one specific curve spread, and the trade is the gap between that fair value and the market. A fact that doesn't map to a spread is background reading, not a signal.

1. Which supply is actually online

New liquefaction capacity never arrives on its start date. A useful first question for any project is whether it is online, ramping or delayed, and the answer should come from output, not from the operator's guidance.

  • Measure the run rate. Take monthly exports from ship-tracking data, annualise them (tonnes × 365 / days in the month) and compare with nameplate. I use mechanical thresholds: at least 85% of nameplate is online, 50–85% is ramping, below that is delayed or still commissioning. An open unplanned outage overrides all three.
  • Expansions hide inside running plants. Project databases often keep one row per site. An expansion at a plant that is already running has no row of its own, so measure it as output above the site's run rate in a year before the expansion.
  • Loadings are not supply. A cargo loaded is not a cargo delivered to the market. When a chokepoint closes, a plant can keep loading for buyers on its own side of it. Those cargoes count as exports but never reach anyone else. Split exports by destination before calling anything global supply.

2. Feedgas from public pipeline postings

Cargo loadings are lumpy: one ship every week or two tells you little about this week. Feedgas, the gas a plant takes in each day, moves first and moves daily. Commercial services sell it, but the raw source is public. In the US, every interstate pipeline must post, for each gas day, the scheduled quantity at each of its delivery points. The point that delivers into the LNG terminal is the plant's nominated feedgas.

Two things I got wrong or nearly wrong:

  • Compare feedgas with feedgas capacity, not with LNG output. A plant burns or strips roughly 8–11% of the gas it takes in. A train rated at 0.7 Bcf/d of LNG output (the EIA rating for each of Golden Pass's three trains) needs about 0.79 Bcf/d of feedgas at 100%. Dividing feedgas by the output rating overstates utilisation.
  • Scheduled is not metered, and the history disappears. The figure is the last nomination cycle, so single days swing and today's value is provisional. The postings site keeps only about 90 days, so a scraper has to run regularly and add to its own file to build any history.
feedgas capacity  ≈ LNG output rating / (1 − fuel and shrink)
utilisation       = scheduled feedgas / feedgas capacity
1 Bcf/d of LNG    ≈ 7.5 Mt/y

3. The project pipeline: post-FID and pre-FID

Capacity under construction (post-FID) is committed; planned capacity (pre-FID) is an option that may never be exercised. Three rules for reading a project list:

  • Lead times cap how soon pre-FID capacity can arrive. An onshore plant takes about 4–5 years from FID to first LNG, a floating one about 3. A pre-FID project dated earlier than that is a placeholder.
  • Databases lag decisions. Status fields update late. Check any FID against the company's own release before relying on it.
  • Diff snapshots to catch FIDs. Few databases record an FID date, but saving the list regularly and comparing statuses shows each project as it moves from planned to under construction.

4. What the curve already prices

Before forming a view, read the one the market already holds. Average the curve into seasons, winter as November to March and summer as April to September, and look at two things:

  • The winter premium. Normally each winter trades above the summer before it. A winter priced below the preceding summer means the market expects supply growth to outrun heating demand. That is where the curve dates the switch from tight to loose.
  • The premium over a reference date. After a supply shock, compare today's curve with the curve on the last day before the shock. The difference, month by month, is how long the market expects the shock to last.
premium(month) = curve today − curve on the pre-shock date
premium(month) ≈ P(disruption still in place in that month) × full-disruption premium + residual

If the front of the curve prices the disruption in full, dividing each later month's premium by the front premium gives an upper bound on the probability the market assigns to the disruption lasting that long. It is an upper bound because some premium survives even after the disruption ends (damaged capacity, delayed projects), and because a winter shortage is worth more than a summer one.

6. Paper-trading discipline

A view becomes a paper trade only with the same fields every time, written down before entry: thesis, catalyst, instrument, entry, target, stop, size, and what would prove it wrong. Mark it daily to exchange settlements and size it from the measured sensitivities, not from conviction.
