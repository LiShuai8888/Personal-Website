Gaussian conjugate priors: the posterior for an unknown mean

A revision note built from working through one slide in the ML-foundations deck (Bayesian Statistics → Gaussian distribution, variance known). The slide states the prior, states the posterior, and bridges them with "then we can show that" — which is exactly the part I could not reproduce from memory. So this note does the work. It also closes the open item I left on the Beta–Bernoulli note: derive the posterior itself instead of quoting conjugacy. Same programme, continuous case.

The one-line version: for a Gaussian with known variance, precisions add — \(1/\sigma_N^2 = 1/\sigma_0^2 + N/\sigma^2\) — and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted blend of prior mean and sample mean. Which means the prior is worth exactly \(\kappa = \sigma^2/\sigma_0^2\) imaginary observations, and nothing else about it matters.

The setup (what the slide assumes)

  • \(\mu\) — the true, unknown mean. The thing we are uncertain about. Unlike the coin's \(\mu\) in the Beta note, this one lives on all of \(\mathbb{R}\), which is why the prior is Gaussian rather than Beta.
  • \(\sigma^2\) — the variance of a single observation. Assumed known. This is the strong, slightly artificial assumption that makes the slide one line long.
  • \(\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}\) — the data, iid \(\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2)\).
  • Prior: \(p(\mu) = \mathcal{N}(\mu \mid \mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\), written out in full because the whole derivation is just this times the likelihood:

$$p(\mu) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_0^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\mu-\mu_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2}\right)$$

  • \(\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n x_n\) — the sample mean.

Worth pausing on: \(\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2)\) and \(\mathcal{N}(\mu \mid \mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) are the same function, used for two different jobs. In the likelihood, \(x\) is the variable and \(\mu\) is a parameter. In the prior, \(\mu\) — a parameter of the model — is itself the variable, and \(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2\) are hyperparameters. Writing a distribution over a parameter is the move that makes this Bayesian at all; a frequentist never writes \(p(\mu)\) because \(\mu\) is a fixed unknown, not a random variable.

Step 1 — posterior ∝ likelihood × prior

$$p(\mu \mid \mathcal{D}) \;\propto\; \left[\prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{N}(x_n \mid \mu, \sigma^2)\right]\mathcal{N}(\mu \mid \mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$$

Written out completely, before any tidying:

$$p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mu)\,p(\mu) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-N/2}(2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2 - \frac{1}{2\sigma_0^2}(\mu-\mu_0)^2\right)$$

The \(\propto\) throws away every factor without a \(\mu\) in it — including that whole prefactor. We are allowed to, because we already know the answer is a Gaussian; once its mean and variance are identified, its normaliser is determined.

Step 2 — take logs, keep only the μ terms

$$\ln p(\mu\mid\mathcal{D}) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2 \;-\; \frac{1}{2\sigma_0^2}(\mu-\mu_0)^2 \;+\; \text{const}$$

The result is already proved at this line, and this is the insight I had been missing. Both terms are quadratic in \(\mu\); a sum of quadratics is a quadratic; so the log-posterior is a quadratic and the posterior is \(\exp(\text{quadratic})\) — a Gaussian. Everything after this is bookkeeping to find which Gaussian. Conjugacy here is not a lucky algebraic coincidence, it is the closure of quadratics under addition.

Step 3 — collect powers of μ

$$\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2 = N\mu^2 - 2\mu\sum_n x_n + \sum_n x_n^2, \qquad (\mu-\mu_0)^2 = \mu^2 - 2\mu\mu_0 + \mu_0^2$$

Using \(\sum_n x_n = N\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}\) and gathering:

$$-\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\left[\frac{N}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}\right]}_{\mu^2\ \text{coefficient}}\mu^2 \;+\; \underbrace{\left[\frac{N\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}}{\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2}\right]}_{\mu^1\ \text{coefficient}}\mu \;+\; \text{const}$$

Step 4 — match coefficients against the target

A Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mu \mid \mu_N, \sigma_N^2)\) has log

$$-\frac{1}{2\sigma_N^2}(\mu-\mu_N)^2 = -\frac{1}{2\sigma_N^2}\mu^2 + \frac{\mu_N}{\sigma_N^2}\mu \underbrace{- \frac{\mu_N^2}{2\sigma_N^2}}_{\text{no }\mu\ \Rightarrow\ \text{const}}$$

Two quadratics are equal iff their coefficients match, so read the answer straight off:

$$\frac{1}{\sigma_N^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{N}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \frac{\mu_N}{\sigma_N^2} = \frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{N\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}}{\sigma^2}$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \mu_N = \sigma_N^2\left(\frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{N\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}}{\sigma^2}\right)$$

That is the derivation. Completing the square is the same computation in different clothing. Note that the natural output is in precisions (reciprocal variances), which is why the slide's version of \(\mu_N\) looks uglier than the thing it is.

Step 5 — bend it into the slide's form

Invert the precision equation, \(\sigma_N^2 = \dfrac{\sigma_0^2\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + N\sigma_0^2}\), substitute, and divide numerator and denominator of each term by \(N\):

$$\mu_N = \frac{\sigma^2/N}{\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2/N}\,\mu_0 \;+\; \frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2/N}\,\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}$$

which is the slide verbatim. The two weights sum to 1, so \(\mu_N\) is a genuine convex combination: it always lands between \(\mu_0\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}\), never outside.

The reason \(\sigma^2/N\) shows up next to \(\sigma_0^2\) is worth saying out loud, because it is the whole intuition. \(\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{x_1+\cdots+x_N}{N}\right) = \sigma^2/N\) — that is the variance of the sample mean. So the formula is blending two independent estimates of the same quantity, the prior mean (uncertainty \(\sigma_0^2\)) and the sample mean (uncertainty \(\sigma^2/N\)), weighting each inversely to its variance. The noisier estimate gets down-weighted. That is all it is.

Reading 1 — information adds

$$\frac{1}{\sigma_N^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{N}{\sigma^2}$$

The cleanest statement of the whole result. The prior carries \(1/\sigma_0^2\) units of information, each observation carries \(1/\sigma^2\), and \(N\) of them carry \(N/\sigma^2\). Two consequences:

  • \(\sigma_N^2\) shrinks with \(N\) regardless of what the data says. In this model you cannot become less certain about \(\mu\) by seeing more of it. (That stops being true the moment \(\sigma^2\) is also unknown — see the open items.)
  • Sequential updating is free. Process one point at a time, using each posterior as the next prior, and you land on the identical answer, because addition is associative. I checked this numerically rather than trusting it; see below.

Reading 2 — the prior is κ pseudo-observations

Set \(\kappa = \sigma^2/\sigma_0^2\). The entire slide collapses to:

$$\mu_N = \frac{\kappa\,\mu_0 + N\,\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}}{\kappa + N}, \qquad \sigma_N^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{N + \kappa}$$

\(\kappa\) is the prior's effective sample size: the prior is worth exactly \(\kappa\) imaginary observations parked at \(\mu_0\). A prior with \(\sigma_0^2 = \sigma^2/10\) is you claiming to have already seen ten data points. That is the honest way to ask whether a prior is defensible — not "does this feel about right" but "am I willing to say this is worth ten prints."

This is the same object as \(\alpha + \beta\) in the Beta–Bernoulli note, where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) were pseudocounts. Discrete rate, continuous level, one idea.

Gap I hit #1 — why is there no summation sign in p(μ)?

Mid-session I asked whether \(p(\mu)\) has no \(\sum\) because \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are known. That is the wrong reason, and getting it right is genuinely clarifying.

A summation appears in a log-density only when you have a product of independent factors, one per observation: \(\ln\prod_{n=1}^N(\cdot) = \sum_{n=1}^N \ln(\cdot)\). The prior is one factor about one scalar. There is no index to run over — no \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_N\) — so there is nothing to sum.

Test the "known" theory against the likelihood: \(\sigma^2\) is known there, and \(\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2\) still has a sum. So knowing a parameter plainly does not remove a summation. What "known" actually buys is different: it makes terms like \(-\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma_0^2)\) constants that can be dropped. That deletes terms, not the summation sign.

Sum ⇔ counting. Constant ⇔ knowing. Two separate mechanisms, easy to conflate.

Confirmation from the other direction: make \(\mu\) a \(D\)-dimensional vector with prior \(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu\mid\boldsymbol\mu_0,\Sigma_0)\) and a sum does appear, inside the quadratic form \((\boldsymbol\mu-\boldsymbol\mu_0)^{\!\top}\Sigma_0^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu-\boldsymbol\mu_0) = \sum_i\sum_j(\cdot)\). The parameter is exactly as unknown as before. The sum arrived because \(\boldsymbol\mu\) became \(D\) components — it counts dimensions now instead of data points, but it is still counting.

Where did the likelihood's sum go, then? The posterior has no summation sign either, yet it absorbed all \(N\) observations. It is still there, compressed into two numbers: \(N\) and \(\sum_n x_n\). Those are the sufficient statistics. Once you have them you can delete the raw data and still compute the exact posterior — a thousand observations collapse to a count and a total. That is the real reason updating stays cheap forever, and it is the same fact as \(\kappa\) and \(N\) being the only things in Reading 2.

Gap I hit #2 — why does μ_N² become "const"?

In Step 4, expanding \(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_N^2}(\mu-\mu_N)^2\) produces a third piece, \(-\mu_N^2/(2\sigma_N^2)\), which gets swept into const. But \(\mu_N\) is the thing we are trying to find — how can it be constant?

Because two different senses of "constant" are in play, and only one matters here:

  • Unknown vs known — \(\mu_N\) is a fixed number whose value we have not worked out yet.
  • Constant vs variable — \(\mu_N\) does not vary with \(\mu\).

Collecting powers of a polynomial only cares about the second. Fixed-but-unknown is constant enough. (Same distinction as gap #1, which is why they belong in one note.)

And we are entitled to discard it, not merely park it. Count unknowns: \(\sigma_N^2\) and \(\mu_N\), two of them, and the \(\mu^2\) and \(\mu^1\) coefficients give two equations. The \(\mu^0\) equation is surplus — and worse than surplus, it carries no usable information, because back at Step 1 the \(\propto\) deliberately destroyed the \(\mu\)-independent factor. In log space that is an unknown additive \(\mu^0\) term. We corrupted that coefficient on purpose; matching it afterwards would be matching against something we threw away.

Which exposes the one real hazard in this notation:

const on the left is not the same number as const on the right. People write "+ const" on both sides of Step 4 and read it as an equality. It is not. Only the \(\mu\)-dependent coefficients are being equated.

Nothing is lost, because normalisation pins the true constant down afterwards: once you know a density is Gaussian with mean \(\mu_N\) and variance \(\sigma_N^2\), its constant is forced to be whatever makes it integrate to 1.

If you ever do want that constant, the gap between the two consts is exactly \(\ln p(\mathcal{D})\), the marginal likelihood. Equating the \(\mu^0\) terms honestly gives

$$\ln p(\mathcal{D}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma_N^2) - \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma_0^2) - \tfrac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{\mu_N^2}{2\sigma_N^2} - \frac{\mu_0^2}{2\sigma_0^2} - \frac{\sum_n x_n^2}{2\sigma^2}$$

the model evidence, used for model comparison and for tuning hyperparameters. So the \(\mu^0\) term is not worthless — it answers a different question from "what is the posterior over \(\mu\)", and you only pay for it when you ask.

Sanity checks

The four corners, which are faster to check than the algebra is to re-derive:

case posterior mean posterior variance reading
\(N = 0\) \(\mu_0\) \(\sigma_0^2\) no data, posterior = prior
\(N \to \infty\) \(\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}\) \(\to 0\) data swamps any proper prior
\(\sigma_0^2 \to \infty\) \(\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}\) \(\sigma^2/N\) flat prior reproduces the frequentist answer exactly
\(\sigma_0^2 \to 0\) \(\mu_0\) \(0\) dogmatic prior; no amount of evidence moves it

Row 3 is the useful one: the classical estimate is the Bayesian one under an uninformative prior, and \(\sigma^2/N\) reappears as the posterior variance. Row 4 is the warning label on strong priors.

Numeric check

Deriving it is not the same as trusting it, so I checked the closed form against a posterior built by brute-force grid integration — no conjugacy used anywhere:

mu0, sd0, sd = 0.10, 0.05, 0.20          # prior mean/sd, known obs sd
x = np.array([0.31, -0.12, 0.44, 0.05, 0.28, -0.03,
              0.51, 0.19, -0.08, 0.36, 0.22, 0.14])
N, mle = len(x), x.mean()

sdN2 = 1 / (1/sd0**2 + N/sd**2)                       # precisions add
muN  = sdN2 * (mu0/sd0**2 + N*mle/sd**2)

g    = np.linspace(-2, 2, 4_000_001)                  # brute force, no conjugacy
logp = -0.5*((g-mu0)/sd0)**2 + (-0.5*((x[:,None]-g[None,:])/sd)**2).sum(0)
p    = np.exp(logp - logp.max()); p /= p.sum()
mu_num, var_num = (g*p).sum(), ((g-(g*p).sum())**2 * p).sum()

N=12  mle=0.1892  kappa=16.0  w_prior=0.5714
closed form   muN=0.138214  sdN2=0.00142857
slide form    muN=0.138214
kappa form    muN=0.138214  sdN2=0.00142857
grid numeric  muN=0.138214  sdN2=0.00142857
sequential    muN=0.138214  sdN2=0.00142857
max abs err vs grid: mu 5.55e-17   var 4.34e-19

All four routes agree to floating-point noise: the precision form, the slide's weighted form, the \(\kappa\) form, and one-observation-at-a-time sequential updating all land on 0.138214, and the grid posterior confirms it to \(6\times10^{-17}\).

The number to sit with is w_prior = 0.5714. Here \(\kappa = 0.04/0.0025 = 16\), so the prior outweighs 12 real observations: the estimate ends at 0.1382, closer to the prior 0.10 than to the sample mean 0.1892. Twelve data points and the data still loses. If that feels wrong, the prior was too tight — \(\sigma_0 = 0.05\) was a claim to sixteen prior observations, and I should have priced it that way before choosing it.

Trading hook

This is the shrinkage estimator, and it is the right tool any time a mean has to come off a short, noisy sample: a spread's fair value from a handful of prints, a strategy's edge from one quarter, a basis level from thin liquidity. \(\kappa\) is the dial for how hard to pull toward the anchor, and Step 5 guarantees you can never pull past it.

Division of labour with the sibling note: Beta–Bernoulli for a rate (win rate, hit rate — bounded on [0,1], updated with pseudocounts), Gaussian for a level (a spread, a return, a basis — unbounded, updated with pseudo-observations).

It also turned out to be a formula I had already written down without recognising it. The Kaggle plan's Week 3 has target-encoding smoothing as (n·category_mean + m·global_mean) / (n + m). That is \(\mu_N\) with \(\kappa = m\) — a Gaussian conjugate posterior mean with the global rate as prior. Verified as the same arithmetic:

target-enc: n=3 cat_mean=0.6667 global=0.4130 m=20 -> 0.4461
conjugate with kappa=m:                              0.4461

So "tune the smoothing constant m" and "decide how many observations my prior is worth" are one question, and the bias-variance framing and the pseudo-observation framing are the same trade-off seen from two sides.

Status

Closed — I can state these without looking them up:

  • The posterior is Gaussian because a sum of quadratics is a quadratic; that is the whole proof, and it lands at Step 2 before any algebra
  • Match the \(\mu^2\) and \(\mu^1\) coefficients; \(\mu^0\) is surplus and was corrupted by the \(\propto\) on purpose
  • Precisions add: \(1/\sigma_N^2 = 1/\sigma_0^2 + N/\sigma^2\), so certainty only ever accumulates and sequential updating is exact
  • \(\mu_N\) is the precision-weighted convex blend of \(\mu_0\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}}\), with \(\sigma_0^2\) against \(\sigma^2/N\) — the variances of the two estimates being blended
  • \(\kappa = \sigma^2/\sigma_0^2\) is the prior's effective sample size; the whole slide is \(\mu_N = (\kappa\mu_0 + N\mu_{\mathrm{MLE}})/(\kappa+N)\)
  • Sum ⇔ counting, constant ⇔ knowing; and \(N, \sum_n x_n\) are the sufficient statistics

Open — next:

  • [ ] Drop the "\(\sigma^2\) known" assumption. In markets it never is. The conjugate pair becomes Normal-Inverse-Gamma and the posterior over \(\mu\) becomes a Student-t — fatter tails, and the "certainty only accumulates" property dies. This is the single most important gap in the note
  • [ ] Derive the posterior predictive \(p(x\mid\mathcal{D}) = \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_N,\ \sigma^2 + \sigma_N^2)\) — irreducible noise plus residual uncertainty about the mean. This is the exact Gaussian analogue of what the Beta–Bernoulli note computes, so the two notes should meet here
  • [ ] Multivariate case: precision matrices add, \(\Sigma_N^{-1} = \Sigma_0^{-1} + N\Sigma^{-1}\). Confirm, and see what breaks when \(\Sigma\) is ill-conditioned
  • [ ] Tune \(\kappa\) on real data rather than asserting it: cross-validate m for target encoding on a Kaggle categorical and check smoothing actually beats the raw category mean
  • [ ] Use the evidence. I wrote \(\ln p(\mathcal{D})\) down and have never used it — empirical-Bayes tuning of \(\sigma_0^2\) by maximising it is the obvious first application

Distinction I keep having to re-make: \(\sigma^2\) is the spread of the observations — a fact about the world, fixed, known here. \(\sigma_N^2\) is the spread of my belief about \(\mu\) — a fact about my state of knowledge, and it collapses toward zero as data arrives. Conflating them is the standard error with this material, and the posterior predictive above is where the difference becomes unavoidable, because there the two simply add.

Related: the discrete counterpart is the Beta–Bernoulli note — the same shrinkage blend for an unknown rate rather than an unknown level, with \(\alpha+\beta\) playing the role \(\kappa\) plays here. Its subject, the posterior predictive, is exactly the piece this note still owes. And any claim that smoothing a thin category mean actually helps has to be measured through the out-of-fold harness in the cross-validation note — a smoothed feature fitted on all the data leaks, and the leak flatters it.
